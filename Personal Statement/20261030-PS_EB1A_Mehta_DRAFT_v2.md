Petitioner’s Statement Regarding OCMS
I have been working in the field of glycoscience (study of carbohydrates) for over 14 years during parts of my PhD, my Post Doc and now my Staff Scientist and Instructor positions. During this period, my expertise helped me build the tools that other scientists use to study glycans — the sugar/carbohydrate chains that coat most proteins on the surface of our cells and that govern infection, immunity, inflammation, and blood-group compatibility and play vital roles in auto-immune diseases, virology and cancer. The central difficulty of my field is that glycans are difficult to work with and producing them is costly and rare. These precious glycans therefore need a miniaturized way of being used for detection of binding of proteins to conserve them. The platform that made such measurement possible at scale is the glycan microarray, which when I started was expensive to build, disposable after a single use, and produced data that few laboratories could analyze. 

My contributions at the National Center for Functional Glycomics (NCFG) at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) – Harvard Medical School (HMS), address each part of that problem: a chemistry process that lets precious glycan microarrays be reused 8 to 15 times instead of once using microwaves (MAWE) [1]; the free software that turns glycan microarray spreadsheets into interpretable data with easy-to-understand symbol drawing for structure interpretation (GLAD and GlycoGlyph) [2,3]; and a synthetic method (Glyco-SPOT) that miniaturizes the production of glycosylated peptides so they can be produced in a cost efficient manner and be screened reasonably [4-6]. These are not parallel inventions; together they form one system, and that system now operates as infrastructure at the National Center for Functional Glycomics (NCFG) which offers glycan microarrays as a service to the greater scientific community. 

My regeneration method is the published standard operating protocol of the National Center for Functional Glycomics, the federally funded national resource where I am key personnel [7]; independent groups around the world publish their results generated with that protocol and analyzed with my software [8-11]; and two of the world's leading carbohydrate-chemistry laboratories have built their own glycopeptide synthesis on Glyco-SPOT [12–13], while biotechnology companies use the same platform — arrays, regeneration chemistry, and analysis software — to test their own products [14–16]. Below I describe three projects — the reuse chemistry, the analysis software, and the synthesis method — each in the same three parts: what I made, why it matters, and the specific documented instances in which others have relied on it.
1: Developments in Glycan Microarrays
A. My work
A glycan microarray is a glass slide presenting hundreds of distinct glycans in thousands of microscopic spots. In order to print these slides requires expert training in chemistry (surface chemistry), glycochemistry (for carbohydrate linker chemistry) and expertise in running microarray printers in a clean environment. Following this, the slides must be carefully blocked to avoid non-specific interactions and stored. These are essentially biological “microchips” to interrogate samples and must be handled in a very clean manner, avoiding dust and careful calibrations so that each spot is accurate within micrometers. I produce these glycan microarrays in thousands of counts for the NCFG with my expertise in chemistry, biochemistry and programmatic skills to operate the microarray printer. 

Once slides are produced, we incubate a biological sample — this can be either patient serum, a potential therapeutic antibody, a purified protein or lectin or many other types of samples — on the slide, and whatever binds to it is detected, revealing which glycans the sample of interest recognizes. The economics are brutal. Printing and quality-controlling each slide costs real money, and the glycans themselves are frequently the scarce reagent costing hundreds to thousands of dollars for micrograms to milligram quantities for each compound. On our largest microarray we have 500 such glycans printed so the cost really adds up. We have 10 publicly available arrays but have published over 20 different types during my time at our center which I am directly responsible for creating, maintaining and running experiments on. This is why expertise and miniaturization are so essential. Furthermore, until my method, one experiment consumed the slide: after a successful assay it was thrown away, even when the same slide was needed to test a second sample, because no chemistry existed that could strip bound material away without destroying the glycans beneath.

With my colleagues at the NCFG, I developed MAWE — microwave-assisted wet erase — a regeneration chemistry that strips bound biomolecules from a glycan microarray and returns the slide to working condition. Our Glycobiology paper demonstrates a single array reused 8 to 15 times, against a prior art of one, with slides durable six weeks in storage and three months in active rotation [1]. I am the person at the NCFG responsible for the glycan microarrays themselves — their production, operation, quality, and the data they generate — as key personnel of the center on grants from the NIH and Department of Defense total worth $8.5 million [17].

B. Why it matters
The direct effect of my work is that the field's most expensive measurement became an order of magnitude cheaper per experiment in consumed glycan material by the ability of reusing the glycan microarrays. More importantly, it enables us to offer these limited microarrays to more people to investigate more biological binding to them and hence increasing opportunities for discoveries. That changes what questions get asked: where previously we could analyze 1 sample, now we could do 8-10. The effect I consider more consequential is institutional. Because reuse removed the binding constraint on throughput, the NCFG adopted MAWE as the standard operating protocol it offers to investigators worldwide — the center publishes the MAWE protocol on its website as the assay procedure for biotinylated and tagged samples submitted by external researchers [7]. When a national resource center makes a method its operating procedure, that method stops being one laboratory's technique and becomes how the field performs the measurement.

2: GLAD and GlycoGlyph — free software that makes glycan data usable
A. My work
Two obstacles stand between a glycan experiment and its interpretation. First, a glycan microarray or glycoproteomics run produces a flood of images and numbers that most biologists have neither time nor expertise nor code to process; second, glycan structures are irregular and branched, with linkages and Greek symbols, that no linear formula captures well, that writing one down in a form a computer can reason about is itself unsolved engineering. I built tools for both problems, and both are free. GLAD (the Glycan Array Dashboard) is my first-author web-based platform for interactive analysis of microarray data, built explicitly so that researchers, not engineers, can interrogate their experiments [2]. GlycoGlyph is my first-author web application (open sourced) for drawing, converting, searching, and decoding glycan structures through a simple text syntax, giving the field a way to write a glycan structure the way one writes a word [3].

B. Why it matters
Data without interpretation and understanding can be meaningless. My work on these tools matters because analysis is the bottleneck that decides whether a glycan experiment is worth doing. A microarray generates thousands of binding curves; without interactive filtering, normalization, and visualization, most of that information dies in image files. GlycoGlyph attacks the other half of the problem: glycan structures circulate through databases, publications, and software in mutually incompatible representations, and its text-based notation lets any tool in the chain write and read a structure losslessly. Both tools are openly accessible on the web with no registration barrier, which is why they spread the way they did — not through a commercial channel, but because researchers at other institutions, including groups with no computational staff, could simply start using them. And because the NCFG returns analyzed data to its customers from this same pipeline, the software is not only cited; it is embedded in the service through which the field obtains its glycan-binding data.

C. How my work has been used
Independent laboratories now publish routine use of both tools — at times in the same studies that use my microarray methods, which is why I describe these layers as one platform. As of October 27, 2026, Google Scholar records GlycoGlyph cited by 78 works and GLAD by 64 [21], published in venues including Nature Communications, Chemical Society Reviews, the Journal of Biological Chemistry, Nucleic Acids Research, and Mass Spectrometry Reviews. These are not ceremonial citations: the Guérardel study above processed its data "with GLAD" as named methodology [8]; the Vanderbilt University Medical Center study used GlycoGlyph alongside the MAWE protocol [9]; and the NCFG service pipeline that produced the data in those papers runs this software end to end.

3: Glyco-SPOT — miniaturized synthesis of glycosylated molecules
A. My work
Glycopeptides, where a specific glycan is attached to a specific peptide sequence — are one of the most sought-after biomolecules to study, but these are nearly impossible to obtain because of the scarcity and cost of both glycans and purified peptides. They are either unstable or challenging to synthesize due to their size and complexity. For example, the glycan must be attached at exactly the right place without damaging either half. My work at the NCFG produced Glyco-SPOT: a method for parallel solid-phase synthesis of glycosylated peptides with per-cycle quality control testing process at microgram scales [4-6]. Glyco-SPOT makes up to 50 individual glycopeptides in a single run in parallel at microgram scale, each can be checked as it is made. The products can be printed as glycopeptide microarrays, tying this synthesis directly to the microarray platform of my other work, due to the scale of the synthesis being small and reducing costs and increased efficiency.

B. Why it matters
Before this work, a laboratory wanting a specific glycopeptide generally commissioned it at enormous cost and waited months, or could not obtain it at all; asking, for example, how an antibody distinguishes one glycopeptide from others would be impossible. My method collapsed that barrier: with a small automated synthesis and built-in quality control, individual laboratories can prepare their own validated glycopeptide libraries, and the microarray format screens them with the same consumption economics as the glycan arrays above. Accessible chemistry creates accessible biology — and the reaction of other laboratories to this work is the evidence. These glycosylated peptides could also themselves be important from a therapy standpoint as therapeutic peptides are gaining popularity, hence my method offers a way to quickly and efficiently generate large number of glycosylated peptides for screening on my microarray platform. Using glycosylation for example, could increase the half-life of therapeutic peptides. 

C. How my work has been used
The published reaction to Glyco-SPOT is adoption by two of the strongest chemical-biology laboratories in the world — as a method used, cited, and built upon. Dr. Felix Loeffler's team — working at the Max Planck Institute of Colloids and Interfaces, one of the world's leading centers of carbohydrate chemistry — described in the Journal of the American Chemical Society (2022) that its own glycan-producing method (VaporSPOT) was designed using my Glyco-SPOT work as its foundation [12]; Loeffler states this in his supporting letter in the original petition as well [26]. 

Our work also enabled us to define novel enzymatic pathways for synthesis of certain glycopeptides using enzymes rather than chemistry [4,5]. This knowledge was used by Ma W. et al (JACS, 2022) where they stated “Glycosyltransferases including β1,3-galactosyltransferase 1 (C1GalT1), α-2,6-sialyltransferases (ST6GalNAc1, ST6Gal1, and Pd2,6ST), α2,3-sialyltransferases (ST3Gal1 and ST3Gal4), β-1,6-GlcNAc-transferase 1 (GCNT1), and β1,4-galactosyltransferase 1 (B4GalT1) have been identified to be effective for synthesizing O-GalNAc oligosaccharides. (10,51−53) Therefore, it was expected that the GalNAc moiety of 1 could be extended by these glycosyltransferases to afford complex glycopeptides 2–16.” directly, citing our discovery (reference 10 in their publication) as the foundation for their use [13]. They also called the method “…elegant synthesis methods (7−10)…”. These are papers in which my methods and discoveries are directly leading to newer advances and is foundationally being adopted by laboratories that are on two continents.

Adoption of Glyco-SPOT has continued since, and I was even invited to write a book chapter for Methods in Molecular Biology series titled “Peptide Libraries” by Dr. Ronald Frank (Senior Advisor EU-OPENSCREEN ERIC – Germany) which is now published [6]. 
Together, this ties these three projects together is visible to any reader of the exhibits: I solved the consumption problem at every layer of the pipeline — synthesis (Glyco-SPOT), miniaturization (glycan and glycopeptide microarrays), the reusability of the slide itself (MAWE), and the data (GLAD/GlycoGlyph) — so that all scientists can benefit from our NCFG center.
